And before your brain even computes, you've already said yes. But just as often, we are the ones creating the urgency. You see a message at 10 p.m., and you sacrifice sleep, convinced it needed to be handled ASAP. Today, we install a pattern interrupt to create space between the ask and your response, so you can make a conscious choice about what you are prioritizing. Whenever a new demand comes in, your default is now a deferral. For example, let me check a few things so I can tell you what's possible with that timeline. And when a demand comes from the inside your own head, the deferral sounds more like this. Tension, it often shows up in our face first. So unclench your jaw, drop your tongue down from the roof of your mouth. ## Day 2 Reference Guide

Today's goal is to create a pause between the request and your response. Use this guide to choose for both mental and physical mini-resets

### Mental Pattern Interrupts

When a request comes your way, your brain may say:

-   "They'll be annoyed if I don't answer right away."
-   "I don't want to seem difficult."
-   "It's easier to say yes than explain."
-   "I can just knock this out tonight."
-   "I won't be able to relax until it's done."
-   "If I wait, I'll fall behind."
-   "I should be able to keep up."

Do not argue with those thoughts, but counter them with self-talk like: 

-   "Their urgency is not automatically my emergency."
-   "Someone else's lack of planning does not have to become my immediate problem."
-   "Checking my capacity is responsible, not selfish."
-   "I can be helpful without always being available."
-   "If I say yes too quickly, I may create a bigger problem later."
-   "My brain wants closure, but that doesn't mean this needs to happen now."
-   "I can care about this without fixing it right now."
-   "A rested response will be better than a rushed one."
-   "I do not have to turn every thought or urge into an assignment."
-   "If this still matters tomorrow, I can handle it then."

### Physical Grounding Exercises

When your nervous system is activated, it becomes much harder to think clearly. These resets help you move out of a stress state. 

#### 1\. Longer exhale

Take one slow inhale. Then exhale slightly longer than you inhaled.

-   Inhale for 3 counts.
-   Exhale for 6 counts.
-   Repeat 1-3 times. 

Longer exhales [activate](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6037091/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) your rest and digest response. 

#### 2\. Jaw release

Stress and tension often shows up in the face first.

-   Imagine your face melting to unlock your jaw. 
-   Move your jaw around in a circular motion.
-   Drop your tongue from the roof of your mouth.

#### 3\. Anchor into the present

This pulls you out of worrying about future worries and back to feeling safe now. 

-   Press both feet firmly into the floor. Wiggle or press each of your toes.
-   Press your back into your chair. Feel the support behind you.
-   Relax your thighs into your chair. Feel the support under you. 

#### 4\. Hand on ribs or stomach

This gives your mind something concrete to focus on and shifts attention away from emotional urgency to your body. 

-   Place one hand on your stomach or ribs.
-   Take one slow breath and feel your body expand into your hand.

#### 5\. Object transfer

Under your desk, transfer the excess energy into a small object. 

-   Click a pen cap
-   Spin a ring on your finger
-   Squeeze a stress ball
-   Rub your thumb over the edge of a notebook.

The small repetitive motion gives your nervous system an outlet. 

#### 6\. Ice water or cold exposure

Stress can raise your internal temperature and make you feel flushed, tense, or keyed up. The cold gives your body a quick cooling. 

-   Come prepared before a high-stakes meeting or conversation with a glass or bottle of ice water nearby.
-   Take a long sip when you need a moment to pause

## Day 3: Renegotiate a commitment


- https://www.linkedin.com/learning/setting-boundaries-that-stick-a-5-day-challenge/day-3-renegotiate-a-commitment

I have something controversial to say to kick us off for Day 3 of our 5-Day Setting Boundaries That Stick challenge, and that is this. No is not a complete sentence—in the workplace, that is. Refusing flat-out, declining things too often or too quickly—that's how you get labeled hard to work with. Plus, I know you care about being a team player and a great collaborator. You just want to do it without running yourself ragged. So when you can't outright say no, not doing that, there's two tactics you can try to renegotiate a request and make it work better on terms for you and the other person. The first is the trade-off approach. This is where you turn it into more of a problem-solving discussion around what can be temporarily paused, deprioritized, or slowed down on. So for example, I hear this new project is important. We'll plan to push out x until next month to compensate. Or you might say, to make room for these meetings, I'll hand off these certain tasks to Suzanne. Let me know if that doesn't work. Second is the conditional yes. Here, what you do is you agree, but with the expectation of a limit in the future. This option is great when you want to bank social capital and trust for later. And here's what it sounds like. I can take this on this time, but we can't always accommodate last-minute requests with less than 24 hours' notice. I'll put time on our calendars, though, for after this busy period to get a system in place going forward so we can get ahead of things like this. Here's what you're gonna do today. Pick one of these tactics and apply it to a boundary situation you are facing, whether it is in a meeting, it's over Messenger or via e-mail. Notice, though, how with both of these tactics, the conditional yes, and the trade-off. You are offering a way forward. You are not leaving the person with a dead end and just saying, sorry, can't help you. That is how you protect both your credibility and your bandwidth.

## Day 4: Hold firm when others push back

- https://www.linkedin.com/learning/setting-boundaries-that-stick-a-5-day-challenge/day-4-hold-firm-when-others-push-back

We're here for Day 4 of 5 of the Setting Boundaries That Stick Challenge. And listen, no matter how tactfully you set limits, if you have always been over-accommodating, uber-responsive, then your new boundaries may surprise others. Some people may not like it. The biggest risk when this happens is you caving. You falling into over-explaining, softening your stance so much, you're right back where you started, absorbing everything. So when you encounter someone who doesn't accept your first answer, this is the formula I want you to use. acknowledge-restate-redirect. First you show you heard their concern. Acknowledge. Then you reinforce that the boundary is still in place. Restate. And then you shift focus to a workable solution that keeps things from escalating and proves you are solution-oriented. Redirect. So let's apply this to a last-minute meeting request. You might say, I know the ideal situation is for me to be there live. I'm already committed at that time though. As I said, I can send input in advance or review the notes afterwards. If neither of those work, then let's figure out who else can represent the team on this. Or how about when you have to say, a deadline is not realistic. That might sound like, I'm invested in this going well, which is exactly why I don't want to force it through quickly. I'm suggesting we adjust the scope slightly. We do the analysis first and treat the study design as phase two. Are you on board with that or do you see another option? I want you to look for a moment today to try out this acknowledge, restate, redirect formula. At work, at home, anywhere else. It could be with your partner, your kid, a salesperson. It is all practice for the boundary muscles you are building.

## Day 5: Set digital boundaries for work-life balance

- https://www.linkedin.com/learning/setting-boundaries-that-stick-a-5-day-challenge/day-5-set-digital-boundaries-for-work-life-balance

You made it to day five of the challenge where we are tackling digital boundaries. Because with so much of work online now, it could feel like you're never off the clock. Your brain is actually never off, even on nights and weekends. It sounds counterintuitive, but expectations often beat boundaries. When you have agreements up front, you eliminate the need for a lot of pushback later. That's why your final assignment is to have a chat with your boss or your closest collaborators. Define turnaround times on messages, what projects or clients need an urgent response, and clearly state the limits you have. Something like, I'm generally offline by 5 p.m. to pick up my kids from school. For anything that is truly time-sensitive, please text me. Identify one person you need to have this expectation-setting conversation with and schedule it today. You don't have to have it, just schedule it. Now, before you go, some bonus moves for boundaries in our always-on world. Number one, add working hours to your e-mail signature. For example, I work between 9 a.m. and 6 p.m. Eastern and respond to messages within one business day. Number two, step up your status updates. Instead of just saying busy, say something like deep work on the budget back at 2 p.m. So people have a heads up and know you just aren't missing in action. Number three, confirm receipt. Sometimes you have to be responsive after hours, especially if you work in a field like PR, medical, or healthcare. In those cases, a quick received or got it, we'll look at this first thing Monday, protects your weekend without leaving the other person hanging. And often the thing is, we have to set boundaries with ourselves. So at night, fully turn off your devices, create friction so it is harder to slip back on and just check a few emails. Have something to look forward to, a book, a walk, a show, so you are pulled towards that, not back into your inbox out of fear, boredom, or curiosity.
